Reading this diagram: bar width is proportional to flow (square-root scale, floor of 6px). Dashed red segments mean the gage read zero — or didn't report — at time of fetch. Angostura, the Isleta Diversion Dam, and the San Acacia intake are each physically capable of taking the entire river during low flow.

Methodology: these are the diverting agencies' own published numbers, taken at face value. Nobody — including the districts themselves — comprehensively tracks how much of a given diversion's return flow gets picked up and rediverted further downstream. The point of this piece is showing that repeated capture happens, across both Colorado and New Mexico, under two different legal regimes — not producing a precise mass balance of the river.

Sources: MRGCD OneRain telemetry (mrgcd.onerain.com) · USGS National Water Information System (waterservices.usgs.gov) · USBR HydroData (usbr.gov/uc/water/hydrodata). Data refreshes daily via an automated pipeline; MRGCD and USGS readings are near-real-time, USBR gage and reservoir figures are the most recent day USBR has published (typically one day behind). USBR publishes reservoir releases as a daily acre-foot total rather than a flow rate — the Cochiti and Elephant Butte release figures above are that total converted to an average cfs for the day (÷ 1.9835), which is why they can read a little differently than a live instantaneous dashboard reading at any given moment.
