Upper Rio Grande · water years 1980–2026

How much of the high-country snowpack shows up as river flow?

Each headwater gage is paired with a snow index. The Chama uses Cumbres Trestle alone. The Conejos uses a 50/50 daily average of Cumbres and Wolf Creek Summit. The upper Rio Grande uses a 50/50 average of Wolf Creek Summit and Beartown. Peak SWE (snow water equivalent) and the SNOTEL precipitation gage cover the snow side, taken from the blended daily series. Daily discharge totaled over the water year (Oct–Sep) and the April–July runoff season covers the flow side.

Trend lines are Theil–Sen slopes. They're marked significant when the Mann–Kendall p-value is below 0.05.

Runoff efficiency

Runoff efficiency here means water-year runoff depth (acre-feet spread over the drainage area, in inches) divided by peak SWE of the snow index. It's an index, not a true basin yield. A falling line means less flow for each inch of snow.

Snow retention: peak SWE ÷ Oct–Mar precipitation

This ratio shows how much of the cold-season precipitation is still on the ground at peak. Values above 1 are normal: April snow adds to the peak, and the precipitation gage under-catches wind-blown snow. None of the three indices has a significant trend. Beartown alone does decline (−6% per decade, p=0.002), but blending in Wolf Creek removes that.

Date of peak SWE

All three indices peak earlier now. The shift is significant on the Rio Grande (about 8 days per decade) and the Chama (about 4 days). The Conejos is borderline (p=0.06).

Drought-year scorecard

Expected is the Apr–Jul runoff predicted from peak SWE by a linear fit to every other year (leave-one-out).

Nov–Feb baseflow is the winter mean flow before melt, as a % of normal. It's a rough proxy for the soil and groundwater storage carried into the melt season.

The pattern so far: in 2002 and 2013, winter baseflow came in at 71–94% of normal, and runoff fell 23–51% short of expected. In 2026, baseflow ran 112–145% of normal and runoff came within 4–13% of expected, despite the earliest peak in the record. Wet antecedent conditions seem to have offset some of the losses. Much of that wetness traces to the October 2025 rain event, the spike at the left edge of the 2026 hydrographs, which recharged soils and baseflow before the snow season started. Three years is a small sample, so treat this as a hypothesis worth testing on more years, such as 2000, 2018 and 2025.

Caveats

  • Blends need both sites. A blended day counts only when both sites reported, so the Conejos and Rio Grande indices start in WY1986, when Wolf Creek came online.
  • Blends fit at least as well as single sites. For WY1986–2026, the r² of WY runoff against peak SWE was:
    • Mogote: Cumbres 0.65, Wolf Creek 0.80, the blend 0.81.
    • Del Norte: Wolf Creek 0.53, Beartown 0.59, the blend 0.59.
    The best weights, about 25% Cumbres and 30% Wolf Creek, gain only 0.01.
  • Flows are gaged, not naturalized. Platoro Reservoir sits above Mogote, and Rio Grande Reservoir plus small transbasin imports sit above Del Norte. Storage carryover can move water between years. La Puente has no large reservoir upstream.
  • Data sources. USGS daily values run through Dec 2024 for the two Colorado gages. 2025–26 come from Colorado DWR, and recent values are provisional.
  • Water year 2026 is an outlier in every index. Peaks came in early March, and only 0.56–0.79 of the Oct–Mar precipitation was still on the ground at peak. That pulls the retention trends down.

Sources: NRCS AWDB REST API (WTEQ, PREC daily); USGS NWIS daily discharge 08284100, 08246500, 08220000; Colorado DWR CONMOGCO, RIODELCO. Water years need ≥250 days of SWE and ≥360 days of flow to count.
