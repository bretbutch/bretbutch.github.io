SUGGESTED CAPTIONS (one line under each chart)
  1. Comparing the first and last 20 years of the shared record, median peak snowpack fell 18% on
     the Rio Chama, 13% on the Conejos and 15% on the upper Rio Grande; median water-year flow fell
     27%, 6% and 1%. Each river delivered about what its smaller snowpack predicts.
  2. Each dot is one water year. In all three basins, recent years mostly fall among earlier years
     with similar snowpacks; more of them sit at the low-snow end.
  3. Water-year flow at La Puente since 1956. WY2026 (provisional) was 80 thousand acre-feet, about
     35% of the 1991–2020 median of 228.
  4. For the snow that fell in 2007–2026, the Colorado headwaters delivered what the 1987–2006
     relationship predicts. Downstream the gap grows to 15% at Otowi, 26% at Albuquerque and 33% at
     San Marcial. Elephant Butte then evaporated about 74 thousand acre-feet a year, roughly 15% of
     what reached it.
  6. Elephant Butte's yearly evaporation tracks the size of the lake (r = 0.98): most in the
     full-lake years, least in the drought years of the 1950s.
  7. At the same storage, the lake now evaporates about a third more than in 1951–1979: the same
     water covers about 14% more surface as sediment fills the lake, and each acre loses about
     16% more.
  8. Each acre now loses about 15% more water a year than in 1951–1979, as April–September warmed
     2.3°F. Warmer years evaporate more in both eras, enough to account for roughly a third to
     two-thirds of the rise.
  9. At all four snow sites the recent snowpack peaks lower and is half gone 6–14 days earlier.
     Part of that is simply less snow; for the same snowpack, only Wolf Creek Summit melts
     clearly earlier.
  10. SNOTEL temperatures jumped warmer at Wolf Creek Summit (+2.4°F), Hopewell (+1.7°F) and
      Cumbres Trestle (+0.4°F) when their sensors were replaced; Beartown shows no clear jump.
      With the jumps removed, the four sites warmed about 0.6–0.7°F per decade since 1991, close
      to NOAA's regional record.
  These data show the change, not its cause.


DATA
  Snow: NRCS SNOTEL daily snow water equivalent (AWDB REST API).
        Cumbres Trestle 431:CO:SNTL, Hopewell 532:NM:SNTL, Wolf Creek Summit 874:CO:SNTL,
        Beartown 327:CO:SNTL.
  Flow: USGS daily mean discharge. Rio Chama near La Puente 08284100 (all years).
        Conejos River near Mogote 08246500 and Rio Grande near Del Norte 08220000 through Dec 2024;
        Colorado DWR (CONMOGCO, RIODELCO) from Jan 2025 on, where USGS daily values end.

  Snow index for each gage (daily average of the sites, then the peak of that daily series):
    Rio Chama near La Puente     1/2 Cumbres Trestle + 1/2 Hopewell
    Conejos River near Mogote    1/2 Cumbres Trestle + 1/2 Wolf Creek Summit
    Rio Grande near Del Norte    1/2 Wolf Creek Summit + 1/2 Beartown
  A blended day counts only when both sites reported. Each blend explains flow at least as well as
  either of its sites alone (r-squared of water-year flow on peak SWE: La Puente 0.73 Cumbres, 0.83
  Hopewell, 0.80 blend, WY1987–2026; Mogote 0.65 Cumbres, 0.80 Wolf Creek, 0.81 blend; Del Norte
  0.53 Wolf Creek, 0.59 Beartown, 0.59 blend, WY1986–2026).

DEFINITIONS
  Water year       October 1 – September 30, named for the year it ends.
  Peak SWE         Highest daily SWE of the water year. Needs 250+ days of data before July 1.
  Water-year flow  Sum of daily mean discharge x 1.983471 acre-feet per cfs-day. Needs 360+ days
                   (365 for the La Puente long record).
  Periods          WY1987–2006 vs. WY2007–2026: equal 20-year halves of the record all three
                   basins share. WY1986 is excluded because Wolf Creek's first season is incomplete.
  Chart 3 lines    LOESS: locally linear, frac=0.2, 3 robustness iterations, evaluated at observed
                   years only. EWMA: span=10, adjust=False (trailing only). 1991–2020 median.


SNOW VS. FLOW: WHAT THE CHARTS SAY
  Expected flow. Each river's water-year flow fit against its snow index for WY1987–2006 (straight
  line), applied to the WY2007–2026 snowpacks; 90% range for the 20-year average:
    Rio Chama   −5% vs. expected, inside the range; trend in the gap p = 0.18
    Conejos     +0%, inside; p = 0.74
    Rio Grande  +6%, inside; p = 0.96
  How flow has responded to each index (WY1987–2006 fit, at the means): Rio Chama 1% less snow ≈
  1.5% less flow (intercept −123 kaf; soils, plants and evaporation take their share first);
  Conejos ≈ 0.9%; Rio Grande ≈ 1.0%. In 2007–2026 the Chama behaved as expected (snow −18%, flow
  −27%). The Conejos (snow −13%, flow −6%) and upper Rio Grande (−15%, −1%) held up better than
  their one-for-one history suggests, within normal variation. Possible reasons, not separable
  here: the wider basins kept more snow than the high sites near the Continental Divide show;
  rain, groundwater or reservoir releases (Platoro; Rio Grande Reservoir) made up part of it
  (October 2025 alone pushed Del Norte to about 5,950 cfs); or chance.

  Why no "flow per inch of snow" headline. Where flow swings harder than snow (the Chama), that
  ratio falls in any drier period even when the snow–flow relationship hasn't changed. The page
  compares against each river's fitted relationship instead (the Chama's median per-inch ratio is
  20% lower, 10.7 -> 8.6 thousand acre-feet per inch, even though its flow is within what its
  snow predicts).

  Snow sites are an index. SNOTEL values show how snowy a year was, not how much water a basin's
  snowpack holds: the sites sit where snow is deep and lasts, and much of each basin sits lower
  with less snow per acre.

  Flow alone, any split year. Moving the split anywhere from 1997 to 2016 (data WY1987–2026),
  Rio Chama median flow falls 27–46%; Conejos 4–21%; Rio Grande +2 to −23%. The Chama's decline
  exceeds the larger of the other two by 14–31 points at every split.

THE RIO CHAMA SNOW INDEX
  Why Cumbres + Hopewell. Cumbres stands for the upper Chama; Hopewell, just outside the drainage in
  the Tusas Mountains, stands for the Rio Brazos side. The author has used this pairing for about
  five years from time in these mountains, before this analysis. The gage data agree:
    - Seasonal gages since 2014 (Rio Chama above Chama 08281400, 95 sq mi; Rio Brazos at Fishtail
      Rd 08282300, 129 sq mi): May–Sep, the upper Chama supplied 33–52% of La Puente's flow and the
      Brazos 28–56% (2018 drought: the two together exceeded La Puente by about 7 kaf).
    - In years when Hopewell is snowier relative to Cumbres, the Brazos carries a larger share of
      the flow (rank correlation 0.77, p = 0.01, 10 summers; Bateman 0.66, Chamita 0.54).
    - WY1987–2026, share of La Puente's year-to-year flow explained: Hopewell 0.83, blend 0.80,
      Cumbres 0.73.
  What depends on it. La Puente vs. the snow-based expectation, WY2007–2026, by weighting:
      Cumbres/Hopewell   0/100  +14%   30/70  +1%   50/50  −5%   70/30  −9% (p 0.07)
                         80/20  −10% (p 0.04)   100/0  −13% (p 0.01)
    Only indices that give Cumbres 80% or more show a significant shortfall.
  Other Chama-basin SNOTEL sites (median peak SWE change, 1987–2006 vs. 2007–2026): Cumbres −14%,
    Chamita (8,360 ft, in the drainage) −8%, Bateman −15%, Hopewell −22%.
  With Cumbres alone, the earlier draft's result: flow per inch −25% (9.4 -> 7.1), significant
    downward trend in the gap (p = 0.003–0.027 across 12 variants), robust to split year, the
    driest years and fit form; Cumbres's snow decline matched Wolf Creek's, so not sensor drift.
    These data can't say whether that shortfall is real or an artifact of one site standing for the
    whole basin; the two-site index is the better-supported representation, so the page uses it and
    reports the Cumbres-only result alongside.

  Gage location. La Puente (drainage 480 sq mi) is upstream of Heron and El Vado reservoirs and of
  the Willow Creek confluence, where San Juan–Chama Project water enters (USGS coordinates: La
  Puente −106.633, Willow Creek below Heron Dam −106.706). Its flows include no imported water.


CHART 4: EXPECTED VS. DELIVERED, HEADWATERS TO ELEPHANT BUTTE
  Snow index   Daily mean SWE of Cumbres Trestle, Wolf Creek Summit and Beartown (all three must
               report), peak per water year.
  Expected     For each gage, water-year flow fit linearly against the snow index over WY1987–2006,
               then applied to each WY2007–2026 snowpack and averaged.
  Whiskers     Where the 20-year average would land 90% of the time if the 1987–2006 relationship
               still held (fit uncertainty plus year-to-year scatter; assumes independent years).
  Gages        Colorado headwaters = Del Norte + Conejos near Mogote; Lobatos; Embudo; Otowi;
               Albuquerque; San Marcial = Rio Grande floodway + Low Flow Conveyance Channel.
               USGS daily values (newer USGS Water Data API); Colorado DWR from 2025 at the
               Colorado gages; USBR's provisional winter values there look ice-affected.
  Results      Gap vs. expected: headwaters +3%, Lobatos −8%, Embudo −11%, Otowi −15%, Albuquerque
               −26%, San Marcial −33%. Only Albuquerque and San Marcial are outside their 90% range;
               Otowi is just inside it.
  Robustness   Same pattern with log, quadratic and reversed fits (San Marcial −28 to −33%, Otowi
               −13 to −15%). Every 2007–2026 snowpack fell inside the 1987–2006 range. Using all 40
               years, the shortfall's trend is significant from Otowi down (p ≤ 0.008) and not at
               the headwaters. The snow index explains about half of each year's flow from Otowi
               down, so the chart shows 20-year averages, not single years.
  Imports      San Juan–Chama water (Azotea Tunnel, USBR) is in Otowi and everything below. Median
               imports were 83 vs. 81 thousand acre-feet in the two periods; subtracting each year's
               import from Otowi still gives about the same gap.
  Evaporation  Elephant Butte, Reclamation's daily estimates, WY2007–2026 average 74 thousand
               acre-feet a year (15% of the San Marcial average).

CHARTS 6–8: ELEPHANT BUTTE EVAPORATION (chain/eb_evaporation_charts.py)
  Data         Reclamation HydroData site 1119, all published series: daily evaporation (datatype 25),
               storage (17) and reservoir surface area (89, acres, daily since 1915). Nothing derived.
               Water years 1951–2025 (WY1950 incomplete; WY2026 provisional values incomplete).
               Three odd daily values left as published: two December 1992 entries that follow runs
               of zeros (several days logged at once) and −15 AF on 2024-09-08; none moves a year's
               total by more than 0.4%.
  Rate         Daily evaporation ÷ daily surface area, summed per water year (feet per year).
  Temperature  NOAA nClimDiv April–September mean, NM divisions 5 (Central Valley) and 8 (Southern
               Desert).
  Eras         WY1951–1979 and WY2003–2025: the two low-lake periods, with overlapping storage. The
               full-lake years 1980–2002 have no early counterparts at the same storage.
  Results      Evaporation vs. lake area r = 0.98 (WY1951–2025).
               Matched pairs (average storage within 15%): 104 pairs, recent year higher in all 104;
               medians: evaporation +33%, lake area +14%, rate per acre +16%.
               Rate per acre: 6.74 -> 7.72 ft/yr (+15%); April–September temperature +2.3°F.
               Rate vs. temperature: WY1951–1979 +2.3% per °F (r = 0.53, p = 0.003);
               WY2003–2025 +4.3% per °F (r = 0.61, p = 0.002). Warming × those sensitivities
               covers about 36–68% of the 15% rise.
               Decade means (ft/yr): 1950s 6.95, 1960s 6.78, 1970s 6.51, 1980s 6.24, 1990s 6.70,
               2000s 7.53, 2010s 7.63, 2020s 8.11. The low 1980s are the full-lake years.
  Correction   The earlier draft (chart 5) and a separate pass both estimated lake area from the slope
               of the storage–elevation curve. Against Reclamation's own area series those estimates
               ran 15–20% high in the 1950s–60s (within about 2% since 2000), which made early per-acre
               rates look too low. That produced a spurious "+28–29% per acre", an apparent abrupt
               step, and early rates that seemed not to track temperature (r = −0.01), all pointing
               to a method change. With Reclamation's area, early rates do track temperature and the
               per-acre rise is about 15%. Chart 5 is retired.

  Trends WY1987–2026 (Sen slope, Kendall p < 0.05): half gone earlier at Wolf Creek (−6.5 d/decade),
  Beartown (−4.6) and Hopewell (−3.3), not Cumbres (−2.6, p = 0.17); melt-out earlier at Wolf Creek
  and Beartown. Each extra inch of peak SWE delays the half-gone date about 1.2 days, so part of the
  shift is less snow. Holding peak SWE fixed, only Wolf Creek is still clearly earlier (half gone
  −5.1 d/decade, melt-out −5.6, peak −7.9; all p < 0.001). Dates vary ±11–16 days year to year.
  Why half gone: it is relative to each year's peak (comparable across sites), and unlike melt-out
  it isn't set by the last inch of snow, where sensor noise and small late storms move the date.

  Temperature (chart 10). NRCS SNOTEL daily average temperature, monthly means (25+ days), against
  NOAA nClimDiv monthly temperature for the climate division (CO 5 Rio Grande basin; NM 2 Northern

  test on the deseasonalized difference) is removed month by month after that date if it is
  statistically clear (p <= 0.001), whatever its size. (An earlier 0.5°F size cut-off hid Cumbres's
  small jump; it was dropped.) Window 1991–2025: Hopewell (May 1991, +3.7°F) and Beartown

  The fix uses NOAA's record as the yardstick, which pulls corrected trends toward NOAA's, so it
  can't show whether the summits warmed faster than the region. Beartown needed no correction, so
  its trend is its own record. Small shifts are hard to date: Cumbres's falls in Jul 2007 in this
  window but May 2012 in its full record. A temperature-free check: Wolf Creek melts
  earlier even for the same snowpack (chart 9 notes).

ELEPHANT BUTTE BALANCE CHECK (why San Marcial stays the endpoint)
  Storage change + releases + evaporation should equal what reached the lake. For WY1988–2026 it
  exceeds what passed San Marcial by 45 thousand acre-feet a year on average (in 33 of 39 years),
  the opposite of losses across the delta, and the gap doesn't change with lake level. Rain on the
  lake, ungaged arroyos, or errors in the evaporation and storage numbers must explain it, so this
  balance can't measure losses below San Marcial.

TEMPERATURE TEST (chain/temperature_test.py)
  Hot years deliver less water for the same snowpack at every gage, but hot seasons are also dry
  ones (r = −0.75 with water-year precipitation). With precipitation held fixed, the heat effect
  disappears at the headwaters and holds from Otowi down only across the whole record, not in
  hot-vs.-cool year comparisons. These data can't apportion the cascade between the atmosphere,
  the riverbed and aquifers, and people. WY2026 can be added once NOAA posts September.


LIMITS AND OPEN ITEMS
  1. La Puente gage history around 2000–2002 (relocation, rating change, new diversions) is not
     verified. The USGS station-description pages are retired; ask the USGS New Mexico Water
     Science Center before publishing.
  2. No independent long-record gage exists to cross-check the Chama. Rio Chama above Chama
     (08281400) and Rio Brazos at Fishtail Road (08282300) both start in 2014.
  3. Flows are gaged, not naturalized. Platoro Reservoir is above Mogote; Rio Grande Reservoir and
     small transbasin imports are above Del Norte; irrigation diversions are above all three gages.
  4. WY2025–2026 flows are provisional. Re-run when they are approved.
  5. Del Norte WY2026: this analysis has 399 thousand acre-feet; the existing Del Norte chart shows
     409. Probably different provisional DWR series. Reconcile before publishing both.
  6. Confirm the EWMA settings (span=10, adjust=False) match the existing Del Norte chart.
  7. Ask Reclamation how Elephant Butte evaporation is computed (pan, coefficient, area table).
     With its own area series no method change needs to be invoked, but label the totals as
     Reclamation's estimates.
  8. MRGCD's own diversion telemetry starts in December 2023 (2024: 283, 2025: 234 thousand
     acre-feet). Earlier years exist only for the Cochiti canals (USGS, since 1955).
